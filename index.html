Growth after tree deaths levels off near 41% full

Lines and bands. Aboveground carbon added each year by tree growth on plots with no recorded harvest between visits, by crowding. Blue is all growth. Orange is growth after subtracting the trees that died, including those killed by damage. Bands are 95% intervals. The shaded bar marks the leveling point, where growth after deaths first peaks: 41% full (95% interval 40% to 42%). There, all growth has reached 94% of the highest value on its curve and deaths take 43% of it. The curves cover 25% to 73% full, the central range of the data. Dashed lines mark 35% full (open below) and 60% full (crowded past).

Past 41% full, growth after deaths dips about 8%, then climbs again, and on the densest plots it is higher than at 41%. Those plots are partly survivors, so this is not a prescription for managed forests. No leveling point was found in 15 of 46 ecological regions (9 of 16 in the West), and it did not appear consistently when visits were 7.5 years or less apart or in measurements centered after mid-2013. Another way of tracking crowding between visits put it near 60% full. Draft study by Chivhenge, Weiskittel, Ray, Woodall, D’Amato, and Daigneault, not yet peer reviewed.

Insects drive most of the rise, and fire shows no clear link

Dots and lines. Change in the yearly chance of each kind of damage for a half step up in crowding (for example, from 30% to 80% full), with its 95% interval. A line that crosses zero means no clear link. The rise in overall damage is steeper in the West (26% higher at 60% full than at 30% full, interval 17% to 38%) than in the East (2%, interval 4% lower to 8% higher).

What this means for carbon

New estimate. Damage is expected to move about 39.5 million metric tons of carbon a year from live trees to dead wood across the lower 48 states (95% interval 23.7 million to 58.8 million). That is a transfer to dead pools, not an emission, and it counts aboveground carbon in trees 12.7 cm (5 inches) across and larger.

How these numbers were made

Data. Remeasured plots of the US Forest Service Forest Inventory and Analysis program in the lower 48 states: 228,797 plot visits on 98,525 plots, 1999 to 2026. Each plot's four fixed-area subplots are treated as the sample.

Crowding. Relative density: the stand density index of the live trees divided by the most the site can support. Crowded means 0.6 or more (past 60% full) and open means under 0.35.

State gaps. Raw shares of plot visits with any recorded natural damage by the next visit, not adjusted for forest type, age, or location. Likely ranges are 95% intervals from resampling whole plots 4,000 times, since most plots were visited more than once. States with fewer than 100 open or crowded plot visits are marked as too few plots.

Yearly chance of damage. An annual hazard model with forest type, stand age, ecological section, and the years between visits. The national curve, the causes, and the East and West comparison come from that model, with 95% intervals. State values are the average predicted for the state's plots.

Growth. 85,821 unharvested plots of the US Forest Service Forest Inventory and Analysis program, measured from 1996 to 2026 (276,984 remeasurement intervals, with curves fitted to the 266,635 intervals in the central range of crowding). Yearly aboveground carbon growth is plotted against the average crowding over each interval and smoothed with penalized splines. The leveling point is the first peak of the curve for growth after deaths, with 95% intervals from resampling whole plots 200 times. Shares of plots fuller than the leveling point use each plot's latest visit and its own region's value; the 68% national figure uses the national value. The hexagon map groups plots by their public coordinates into 60 km hexagons and shows only hexagons with at least 20 plots. Crowding here is the average over the interval between visits, a slightly different measure from the damage panels, which use crowding at the first visit.

Carbon. Expected yearly transfer of aboveground live tree carbon to dead pools, built from all forested plots with their sampling weights. The national interval reflects differences among ecological sections.

Limits. These are associations between crowding at one visit and damage by the next, not the effect of thinning a forest. The growth results are also associations: they apply to plots not harvested between visits, they depend on how crowding is tracked between visits, and they are not thinning guidance. The work is not yet peer reviewed. No plot locations are shown or included.
