Tree growth levels off near 41% full, well before a forest is crowded

Lines and bands. Carbon added each year by tree growth on unharvested plots, by crowding. Blue is all growth. Orange is growth after subtracting the trees that died. Bands are 95% intervals. The shaded bar marks where growth after deaths levels off: 41% full (95% interval 40% to 42%). At that point all growth has reached 94% of its peak and deaths take 43% of it, while the yearly chance of damage keeps rising with crowding.

Past about 50% full, growth after deaths changes little and then rises slowly, and it did not level off in every subset of the data. The densest unharvested plots are partly survivors, so this is not a prescription for managed forests. In the West, deaths take 89% of all growth at the leveling point. From a manuscript in preparation by Chivhenge and colleagues, not yet peer reviewed.

What this means for carbon

New estimate. Damage is expected to move about 39.5 million metric tons of carbon a year from live trees to dead wood across the lower 48 states (95% interval 23.7 million to 58.8 million). That is a transfer to dead pools, not an emission, and it counts aboveground carbon in trees 12.7 cm (5 inches) across and larger.

How these numbers were made

Data. Remeasured plots of the US Forest Service Forest Inventory and Analysis program in the lower 48 states: 228,797 plot visits on 98,525 plots, 1999 to 2026. Each plot's four fixed-area subplots are treated as the sample.

Crowding. Relative density: the stand density index of the live trees divided by the most the site can support. Crowded means 0.6 or more (past 60% full) and open means under 0.35.

State gaps. Raw shares of plot visits with any recorded natural damage by the next visit, not adjusted for forest type, age, or location. Likely ranges are 95% intervals from resampling whole plots 4,000 times, since most plots were visited more than once. States with fewer than 100 open or crowded plot visits are marked as too few plots.

Yearly chance of damage. An annual hazard model with forest type, stand age, ecological section, and the years between visits. The national curve, the causes, and the East and West comparison come from that model, with 95% intervals. State values are the average predicted for the state's plots.

Growth. 85,821 unharvested plots of the US Forest Service Forest Inventory and Analysis program, measured from 1996 to 2026 (276,984 remeasurement intervals, with curves fitted to the 266,635 intervals in the central range of crowding). Yearly aboveground carbon growth is plotted against the average crowding over each interval and smoothed with penalized splines. The leveling point is the first peak of the curve for growth after deaths, with 95% intervals from resampling whole plots 200 times. Shares past the leveling point use each plot's latest visit and its own region's value; the 68% national figure uses the national value.

Carbon. Expected yearly transfer of aboveground live tree carbon to dead pools, built from all forested plots with their sampling weights. The national interval reflects differences among ecological sections.

Limits. These are associations between crowding at one visit and damage by the next, not the effect of thinning a forest. The work is not yet peer reviewed. No plot locations are shown or included.
